Research

Entering the Field: American Social Surveys of China in the Republican Era, 1909–1928

M.A. Thesis, Stanford University REGION: China | Advisor: Thomas Mullaney, History https://doi.org/10.25740/jw159fx4601

This research examines how American social surveys of China in the Republican era (1909–1928) were conducted, rather than what they found. Drawing on archival research in the papers of Sidney D. Gamble and John S. Burgess, it unpacks how modern knowledge about Chinese society was produced in practice by integrating materials, institutions, personnel, and local resources. The analysis unfolds across three dimensions. First, at the level of material infrastructure, this research traces how survey equipment functioned, sometimes generated friction, and required adjustment at the intersection of Western knowledge-producing technology and Chinese local conditions. Second, at the level of institutions and organizations, it examines how American transnational missionary networks and educational institutions provided the organizational backbone for survey work, and how Chinese collaborators mediated between American investigative methods and indigenous society. The last section of this research places American surveys within a turbulent socio‑political landscape of civil war, nationalism, and competing imperialisms in modern China. It shows how these structural forces repeatedly redirected American research agendas and ultimately eroded the institutional foundations on which survey production depended. This research concludes that American social surveys of China were never a ready-made toolkit transplanted onto a passive field, but rather contingent and negotiated practices shaped by the enduring tension among objects, organizations, and social realities.
